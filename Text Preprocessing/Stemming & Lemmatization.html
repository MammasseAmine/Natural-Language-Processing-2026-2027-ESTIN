Stemming & Lemmatization

Many words, one meaning
The Problem: One Idea, Many Word Forms

Human language loves variety. The same simple idea can be written in many shapes:

play plays played playing play

To a computer, connect, connected, connecting, and connection look like four totally different words. To a human, they are clearly the same idea. Normalization is the step that squeezes all these forms down to one base form so the machine can see they belong together.

Why bother? The vocabulary payoff. Fewer distinct words means smaller, simpler data for the model. That gives smaller feature vectors, less "empty space" in the data, and models that learn well even from small datasets. If someone searches "connect", they probably also want pages about "connected" and "connections".

A Little Word-Building (Morphology)

Before we cut words, let's see how words are built. A morpheme is the smallest piece of a word that carries meaning. Most words are a stem (the core meaning) plus one or more affixes (prefixes and suffixes that add grammar or change the meaning).

Inflection

Changes grammar but keeps the meaning and word type: run → runs → running → ran. This is what we most want to strip off.

Derivation

Builds a new word, often a new type: organize → organization → organizational. Whether to collapse these depends on the task.

Three words to keep straight Root – the smallest core left after removing every affix (nation in internationalization).
Stem – what a stemming tool spits out; may not be a real word (studi).
Lemma – the proper dictionary word; always valid (study, be, good).

Method 1: Stemming (Fast & Rough)

Stemming chops off the ends (and sometimes fronts) of words using simple fixed rules. It's fast, but it doesn't check a dictionary – so the result is often not a real word.

Popular stemmers

Porter Stemmer

Made by Martin Porter in 1980. The most famous one – rule-based, fast, everywhere. connecting/connection → connect.

Snowball

An improved Porter that also works for many languages.

Lancaster

More aggressive – cuts harder and gives shorter stems. maximum → maxim.

Peek inside Porter (simplified) It counts vowel-consonant patterns, then applies rules in order: -sses → -ss (caresses → caress), -ies → -i (ponies → poni), drop -s (cats → cat), handle -ed/-ing (hopping → hop), -y → -i (happy → happi), then longer suffixes like -ational → -ate.

Good things

  • Very fast and cheap to run
  • Shrinks the vocabulary
  • Great for search engines & indexing

Weak spots

  • Can make non-words (studi)
  • Ignores grammar and context
  • Can merge unrelated words

Two classic mistakes Over-stemming – two different words get the same stem: universe and university both become univers.
Under-stemming – two related words don't get merged: data and datum stay separate.

Method 2: Lemmatization (Slow & Smart)

Lemmatization reduces a word to its real dictionary form – the lemma. Instead of blindly chopping, it uses a dictionary and grammar knowledge, so the result is always a valid word.

Why part-of-speech matters Look at the last two rows. The same word meeting becomes a different lemma depending on whether it's a noun or a verb. A lemmatizer with no grammar info just guesses "noun" and gets verbs wrong – the single most common beginner mistake in NLTK.

How it works – step by step

1

Split the sentence into words (tokenize)

2

POS-tag each word (noun? verb? adjective?)

3

Look up word + tag in the dictionary

4

Return the correct lemma

All this extra checking makes lemmatization slower and heavier than stemming – but the results are linguistically correct.

The same sentence, both ways

Original The students were studying and writing reports.
After Stemming the student were studi and write report
After Lemmatization the student be study and write report

Notice: stemming gave the non-word studi, while lemmatization kept the real word study and correctly turned were → be. Lemmatization keeps meaningful words, making it better for advanced tasks.

Do We Still Need This in 2026?

The big caveat Modern Transformer models (BERT, GPT-family) split text into subwords and learn word forms on their own – so they usually don't need stemming or lemmatization. But these classic tools are far from dead.

Classic Search (BM25 / TF-IDF)

Still leans heavily on lemmatization to find more relevant results.

Low-Resource Languages

Languages without huge training data still need morphological analyzers and lemmatizers.

Interpretability

Lemmatized features are much easier for humans to read than subword pieces.

Edge / IoT Devices

Tiny devices can't run huge LLMs, so lightweight stemmers still do the job.

Key Takeaway

Both stemming and lemmatization collapse many word forms into one, so a computer can see that connect, connected, and connections mean the same thing. Stemming is fast but rough and may make non-words. Lemmatization is slower but smart and gives real dictionary words. Pick speed or accuracy based on what your task needs.
