Feedforward Neural Networks

The smallest deep model – and it lives inside every Transformer
One Direction, No Loops

A Feedforward Neural Network (FNN), also called a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP), is the simplest deep model. Numbers enter on the left, pass through layers, and leave on the right. Nothing ever goes backwards during prediction, and nothing loops. In graph terms it is a directed acyclic graph.

It is worth your time for one reason: it never went away. Transformers, BERT and GPT all contain feedforward layers inside them. Learn this chapter well and half of the Transformer is already familiar.

Hand-Made Features vs Learned Features

In Chapter 6 you built a CRF by writing features yourself. A neural network removes that job.

The old way – you design the features

You wrote rules such as word ends in “-ing”, previous word is “the”, word is capitalised. Hundreds of yes/no switches, invented by hand.

  • Slow to build, and needs a language expert for every language
  • Sparse: almost every value is zero
  • The model can only add weighted scores – purely linear
  • A feature you forgot simply does not exist for the model

The neural way – the network designs them

You give raw dense word vectors. Hidden layers invent their own internal features while training, and combine them in non-linear ways.

  • No feature engineering by hand
  • Dense vectors: similar words share information
  • Captures interactions like “capitalised and not first word”
  • The same architecture serves many languages and tasks

Inside a Single Neuron

Every neuron does the same two things: add up weighted inputs, then bend the result with an activation function.

z = (w₁·x₁ + w₂·x₂ + w₃·x₃) + b   →   a = f(z)

Inputs (x)

The numbers describing your data – one slot per feature.

Weights (w)

How much each input matters. Learned automatically during training.

Bias (b)

A free shift. It lets the neuron fire even when all inputs are zero, moving the decision boundary.

Activation f

The bend. Without it the whole deep network collapses into one linear equation.

    Vanishing gradients. Look at the flat tails of sigmoid and tanh. Where the curve is flat, the slope is almost zero, so almost no learning signal travels back through that neuron. Deep networks built on sigmoid learn painfully slowly. ReLU has a constant slope of 1 on the positive side – that single fact is why deep learning took off.

    Key insight for NLP: BERT and GPT do not use plain ReLU inside their feedforward sub-layers – they use GELU. GELU keeps ReLU’s non-saturating growth but rounds off the corner at zero, and it scores better on most NLP benchmarks.

    How the Network Learns

    Prediction is the easy half. Learning means fixing the weights, and it takes three ingredients: a loss that measures the mistake, backpropagation to find who is to blame, and gradient descent to make the correction.

    1. The loss: cross-entropy

    For classification we use cross-entropy loss. It looks only at the probability the model gave to the correct answer, and punishes low confidence very hard.

    Loss = − ln( pcorrect )

    2. Backpropagation: assigning blame

    The loss is one number at the far right. Backpropagation carries it backwards through the network with the chain rule, working out how much each individual weight contributed to the error. Press play to watch the signal reverse.

    3. Gradient descent: rolling downhill

    Now update each weight in the opposite direction of its gradient. The learning rate decides the size of each step. Too small and you crawl; too large and you bounce past the bottom forever. Optimisers such as SGD and Adam do exactly this, Adam adapting the step size per weight.

    A realistic NLP model with 300-dimensional embeddings, a 5-word window and 500 hidden units already carries (1500 × 500) + 500 = 750 500 parameters in its first layer alone. Widening the hidden layer costs you linearly, and widening the input costs you just as much – which is why extra depth is usually cheaper than extra width.

    Regularization Techniques

    With that many weights, a network can simply memorise the training set. It scores beautifully on data it has seen and badly on anything new – that is overfitting. Regularization is any trick that stops it.

    Dropout

    Randomly zeros neuron outputs during training with probability p. Neurons can no longer rely on one another, so they stop co-adapting. At test time nothing is dropped and the weights are scaled by (1−p).

    Typical: p = 0.1 – 0.5 for hidden layers.

    click for the practical setting

    Weight Decay (L2)

    Adds λₖθₖ² to the loss, which pushes every weight toward zero. Large weights become expensive, so the model prefers smooth, simple solutions.

    In practice: use AdamW, which decouples weight decay from the gradient update properly.

    click for the practical setting

    Early Stopping

    Watch the validation loss and stop the moment it stops improving. The simplest method here, and very often the most effective one.

    Typical: a patience of 5–10 epochs before you call it off.

    click for the practical setting

    Data Augmentation

    Make more training data out of what you have: synonym replacement, back-translation, random deletion or insertion of words.

    Best for: small NLP datasets, where extra examples matter most.

    click for the practical setting

    Batch Normalization

    Rescales the inputs of a layer to zero mean and unit variance, computed across the whole mini-batch. Mainly a stability aid.

    Note: common in computer vision, rare in NLP – batch statistics behave badly on sequences of different lengths.

    click for the practical setting

    Layer Normalization

    Normalizes across the features of each example on its own, so one sentence never depends on the others in the batch.

    Note: the standard in NLP and Transformers – it handles variable-length sequences and any batch size, even a batch of one.

    Strengths and Hard Limits

    Good at

    • Simple architecture, easy to implement
    • Fast to train compared with big sequence models
    • Universal approximation: can fit nearly any function
    • Structured, fixed-length features
    • Solid baseline for topic labelling, language ID, spam filtering

    Cannot do

    • Model order or dependencies between words in a sequence
    • Remember anything – there is no memory mechanism
    • Handle long texts well
    • Accept variable input length – the size is fixed in advance
    • Match Transformers on modern NLP benchmarks

    The one limitation that drives the rest of the course. A feedforward network takes a fixed-size input and has no memory, but language is a sequence of any length with long-distance links. Recurrent networks answer this by carrying a hidden state forward. Attention and Transformers answer it by letting every word look directly at every other word. Both keep the neuron, the activation, the softmax and backpropagation exactly as you learned them here.

    Feedforward Layers Inside BERT and GPT

    Every encoder layer of BERT follows the same four-step recipe. Click a block to see its job.

    Remove the feedforward sub-layers and BERT loses most of its representational capacity. Attention alone only mixes information between positions – it does not transform it.

    Attention decides what information should be used. The feedforward network decides how that information is transformed.

    Many students assume attention does most of the work in a large language model. In reality the feedforward layers hold most of the parameters, burn a large share of the compute, and carry much of the model’s stored knowledge. One more detail worth remembering: inside a Transformer the feedforward sub-layer is a 2-layer network applied independently to every position – the same weights, reused at each token.

    Key Takeaway

    The feedforward network is the smallest deep model, and every later architecture – RNN, LSTM, Transformer – is built from the same four ideas you met here: a weighted sum, a non-linearity, a loss, and gradient descent. What changes later is how information moves between positions. What never changes is the neuron.
