Bag-of-Words

All the Words, None of the Order
What is Bag-of-Words?

Computers cannot read words directly – they need numbers. Bag-of-Words (BoW) is one of the oldest and simplest ways to turn text into numbers.

The idea is simple: look at a document as an unordered "bag" of words. Grammar, word order, and sentence structure are all thrown away. The only thing that is kept is which words appear, and how many times.

Core Idea
Words are treated independently Word order is ignored Only occurrence / frequency counts Each document becomes a number vector
Building a Bag-of-Words Model
1

Collect documents

Gather the texts you want to turn into vectors, e.g. "I love NLP", "I love Machine Learning".

2

Preprocess the text

Lowercase it, split into tokens, remove punctuation and stop words, then stem or lemmatize.
"I Love NLP!" → ["love", "nlp"]

3

Build the vocabulary

List every unique word across all documents, e.g. [love, nlp, machine, learning, useful].

4

Count word frequencies

For each document, count how many times each vocabulary word appears. This gives one vector per document.

Worked Example

Take three short documents:

d1: "the cat sat on the mat"  |  d2: "the dog sat on the log"  |  d3: "cats and dogs are pets"

After lowercasing and tokenizing, the vocabulary (alphabetical) is:

[and, are, cat, cats, dog, dogs, log, mat, on, pets, sat, the]

andarecatcatsdogdogs logmatonpetssatthe
d1 001000 011012
d2 000010 101012
d3 110101 000100

d1 and d2 share four words ("on", "sat", "the" ×2) even though they talk about different animals. d3 shares no exact word with d1 or d2 because "cat" ≠ "cats" and "dog" ≠ "dogs" – this is exactly why stemming and lemmatization matter.

Two Ways to Count

Binary BoW

Only records whether a word appears or not (1 or 0).

  • "NLP" appears → 1
  • "AI" does not appear → 0

Frequency BoW

Stores how many times each word appears.

  • "NLP NLP NLP AI" → NLP = 3, AI = 1
In Simple Math Terms

If the vocabulary has words w₁, w₂, ..., wₙ, then any document becomes a vector of counts:

D = [c₁, c₂, c₃, ..., cₙ]

where cáµ¢ is simply how many times word wáµ¢ shows up in that document.

Advantages
Simple and easy to understand Easy to implement Works well for many classic ML tasks Fast to compute
Limitations
Ignores word order Ignores meaning (semantics) Cannot capture context Produces very sparse vectors Vocabulary can get huge Can't tell synonyms apart

Because word order disappears, two sentences with opposite meanings can end up looking almost identical:

"Dog bites man"
[dog:1, bites:1, man:1]
"Man bites dog"
[dog:1, bites:1, man:1]

Same vector, very different meaning – this is the price BoW pays for its simplicity.

Going Further: N-grams

Plain BoW only counts single words (unigrams), which is exactly what loses the order. A simple fix is to count short sequences of words instead, called n-grams.

1

Unigram

"the", "cat", "sat" – this is standard BoW.

2

Bigram

"the_cat", "cat_sat" – can tell "not good" apart from "good".

3

Trigram

"the_cat_sat" – captures short phrases like "New York".

Bigger n captures more context, but the vocabulary grows fast and the vectors become sparser – so n is rarely bigger than 2 or 3 in practice.

Where BoW is Used
Text Classification Spam Detection Sentiment Analysis Topic Classification Information Retrieval Document Clustering
Key Takeaway

Bag-of-Words turns text into simple count vectors by throwing away grammar and order. It is fast, easy, and still useful today – but because it ignores meaning and context, it is usually a first step, not the final answer, on the road to more powerful NLP methods.
