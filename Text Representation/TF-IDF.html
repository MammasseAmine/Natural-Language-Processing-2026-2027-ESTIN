TF-IDF

Weighting Words That Matter
What is TF-IDF?

Computers cannot read words directly. Before any algorithm can work with text, we must turn words into numbers. TF-IDF (Term Frequency – Inverse Document Frequency) is a simple and popular way to do this. It gives every word in a document a score that shows how important that word is.

Main idea: a word gets a high score if it appears often in one document, but a low score if it appears in almost every document. Common words carry little information; rare, specific words carry a lot.
A bit of history: the idea of weighting terms by how specific they are was introduced by Karen Spärck Jones in 1972. Her paper on term specificity became the foundation of modern search engines.
Why Do We Need It?

Look at three short documents:

D1

"The cat is sleeping on the sofa."

D2

"The dog is sleeping in the garden."

D3

"The cat and dog are playing."

The word "the" shows up in every document. It is frequent, but it tells us nothing special about any single one. The word "cat" appears in only some documents, so it is more useful for telling documents apart. TF-IDF turns this idea into a formula: give common words a low weight, and give distinctive words a high weight.

Term Frequency (TF)

TF measures how often a word appears inside one document.

Normalised TF TF(t, d) = (times word t appears in document d) / (total words in d)

Example – the document "cat cat dog bird cat" has 5 words:

WordCountTF
cat33/5 = 0.6
dog11/5 = 0.2
bird11/5 = 0.2
Inverse Document Frequency (IDF)

IDF measures how rare a word is across the whole collection of documents. A word found in many documents gets a small weight.

IDF IDF(t) = log( N / DF(t) )

Here, N is the total number of documents, and DF(t) is how many documents contain the word t.

Why a logarithm? Without it, a word appearing in just 1 document out of a million would get a weight a million times bigger than a word in every document. That gap is unusable in practice. The logarithm compresses the scale – rarity still counts, but the numbers stay reasonable.
Watch out – the zero-division trap: if a word never appears anywhere in the collection, DF(t) = 0 and the formula breaks. Real systems avoid this with a smoothed version.
Smoothed IDF (used in scikit-learn) IDF(t) = ln( (1+N) / (1+DF(t)) ) + 1

Adding 1 to the top and bottom is like assuming one extra "phantom" document that contains every possible word. This guarantees the formula never divides by zero, and every IDF value stays positive.

Example – corpus of 3 documents:

DocContent
D1cat dog
D2cat bird
D3cat fish

"cat" appears in all 3 documents → IDF(cat) = log(3/3) = 0 → not informative at all.
"dog" appears in only 1 document → IDF(dog) = log(3/1) ≈ 1.098 → clearly more informative than "cat".

Vector Space & Cosine Similarity

Each row of the matrix is a point (a vector) in space. Comparing two documents becomes a geometry question: how close are their vectors?

Cosine similarity cos(θ) = (d₁ · d₂) / (‖d₁‖ ‖d₂‖)

This measures the angle between two vectors, not their length – so document length does not distort the comparison. A value of 1 means the documents point in the same direction (same topic); 0 means they share no vocabulary.

This is exactly how a search engine works: turn the query into a TF-IDF vector, then rank every document by cosine similarity to that vector. The same idea powers document clustering, duplicate-question detection, and plagiarism checks.

L2 Normalisation

TF-IDF vectors can have very different lengths. Dividing each vector by its own length (L2 norm) makes cosine similarity as simple as a dot product. This is the default behaviour in scikit-learn.

L2-normalised vector v = tfidf / ‖tfidf‖₂

Take two documents that talk about the same thing but one is longer. Without normalising, the longer document's vector is simply bigger in every direction – even though it points the same way. Normalising rescales both vectors to length 1, so only direction (topic) is compared, not size.

Same topic, different length → far apart without normalising, identical point after normalising.

Strengths and Limits

Strengths

  • Simple to implement
  • Fast to compute
  • Works well on small and medium datasets
  • Easy to interpret
  • A strong baseline for many NLP tasks

Limits

  • Ignores meaning
  • Ignores context
  • Ignores word order
  • Produces sparse vectors
  • Cannot see that "car" and "automobile" mean the same thing
Where TF-IDF Is Used

Search Engines

Rank documents against a query using cosine similarity.

Keyword Extraction

The highest-scoring words summarise what a document is about.

Text Classification

TF-IDF vectors feed strong baselines for spam and topic detection.

Clustering & Deduplication

Find near-duplicate documents using cosine similarity thresholds.

Recommender Systems

Recommend items with similar TF-IDF descriptions.

Key Takeaway

TF-IDF answers one question for every word: "What makes this document unique inside the corpus?" Frequent-everywhere words fade into the background, and rare, specific words rise to the top. It is simple, fast, and still a strong first step before trying more advanced NLP methods.
