Levels of Analysis in NLP

From sounds to meaning – the layers of human language
What Are "Levels of Analysis"?

In Natural Language Processing, Levels of Analysis are the distinct layers at which we can examine, model, and compute human language. Each level captures a different type of linguistic information – from the physical sound all the way to the speaker's hidden intention.

A good NLP system must often combine multiple levels to truly understand what is being said. This course walks through seven canonical levels, moving from the surface form (sounds) to meaning in context.

Phonological Analysis

Phonology studies the sound system of a language. In speech-based NLP, this is the first step.

Key Concepts

  • Phoneme – the smallest sound unit
  • Stress – emphasis on syllables
  • Intonation – pitch patterns
  • Pronunciation – how words are spoken

Applications

  • Speech Recognition
  • Text-to-Speech Systems
  • Voice Assistants
  • Pronunciation Modeling

Morphological Analysis

Morphology looks at the internal structure of words. Words are built from smaller meaningful pieces called morphemes.

Types of Morphemes

  • Root – the core meaning
  • Prefix – added at the start
  • Suffix – added at the end
  • Infix – inserted inside (in some languages)

Inflection vs Derivation

  • Inflection – changes grammar (walk → walked)
  • Derivation – creates new words (happy → happiness)
Example "unhappiness" un + happy + ness = prefix + root + suffix

Applications: Stemming, Lemmatization, Information Retrieval, Machine Translation

Syntactic Analysis

Syntax studies how words combine to form grammatically correct sentences.

Correct

  • "The student solved the problem."

Incorrect

  • "The problem student solved the."

Parsing identifies grammatical relationships. It builds a tree showing how words connect.

Semantic Analysis

Semantics focuses on the literal meaning of words and sentences.

Objectives:

  • Resolve ambiguity
  • Identify relationships among concepts

Pragmatic Analysis

Pragmatics studies language in context. The intended meaning can be different from the literal meaning.

Teacher "Can you open the window?" literal: question about ability → pragmatic: polite request
Sarcasm "Oh great, another meeting." literal: positive → pragmatic: negative sarcasm

Importance: Understanding intentions, Dialogue Systems, Chatbots, Virtual Assistants

Discourse Analysis

Discourse studies relationships among multiple sentences.

Coreference "John bought a new laptop. He loves it." "He" = John, "it" = laptop

Key Tasks: Coreference Resolution, Topic Tracking, Text Summarization, Dialogue Understanding

Example Pipeline for Question Answering

  1. Text is tokenized and analyzed morphologically.
  2. Sentence structure is parsed syntactically.
  3. Meaning is constructed semantically.
  4. Pragmatic intent is inferred from context.
  5. Answer is selected or generated, considering discourse context.
Do the Levels Still Matter in the LLM Era?

Modern large language models do not have explicit modules for "the syntax stage" or "the semantics stage." They are trained end-to-end to predict the next token. So is the level framework obsolete?

No – it has changed role from blueprint to lens.

What changed: The classical pipeline made each level an explicit, hand-built component. LLMs collapse the pipeline – a single network learns whatever representations help it predict text. Probing studies show these networks rediscover the levels internally: lower layers encode morphology and syntax, higher layers encode semantics and discourse – without ever being told to.
Why you still need the framework:
  • Diagnosis. When a model fails, the levels give you vocabulary: is this a coreference error, a quantifier-scope error, a pragmatic misread? You cannot fix what you cannot name.
  • Evaluation. Benchmarks are organised by level – NLI tests semantics, Winograd tests discourse, sarcasm sets test pragmatics. Understanding the levels is understanding what is being measured.
  • Data & prompting. Knowing where a task sits tells you what context a model needs to solve it.

The levels are no longer how we build systems, but they remain how we understand them. The structure of language did not disappear because we stopped programming it by hand – it simply moved inside the weights.

Key Takeaway

The seven levels of analysis give us a roadmap for understanding human language. Whether we build systems level-by-level or train a single neural network, these layers help us diagnose, evaluate, and improve NLP systems. Language is layered – and so is our understanding of it.

What is WordNet?

WordNet is a large, publicly available lexical database of English. It groups English words into sets of synonyms called synsets, provides short definitions, and records the various semantic relations between these synonym sets.

It acts like a combination of a dictionary and a thesaurus, organized logically to show how words are conceptually linked.
