Hidden Markov Models

From What You See to What's Hidden
The Big Idea

You cannot see the weather from inside your room. But you can see how many ice creams your friend eats. Lots of ice cream usually means a sunny day. No ice cream might mean rain. You never see the weather directly. You only see a clue, and you guess from there. That single idea is the whole engine behind a Hidden Markov Model (HMM): something real is hidden, and you observe something else that depends on it.

Hidden = the weather. Observed = the ice cream count. HMMs are used in NLP (guessing word tags), speech recognition (guessing words from sound), and bioinformatics (guessing gene regions from DNA).

Markov Chains, First

Before "hidden" anything, meet the plain Markov Chain: a system that moves between states, where the next state only depends on the current state, not on everything that happened before. This short-memory rule is called the Markov Property.

P(next state | all past states)  ≈  P(next state | current state)
Everything before the current state gets compressed away. Only "now" matters for predicting "next". This is what makes the model easy to compute.

Add a Layer You Cannot See

In a plain Markov chain, the states are what you see. In an HMM, the states are locked away. Each hidden state only shows you a symbol it "emits", based on its own probabilities. Two different hidden states can even produce the same observation. That is exactly why the state stays hidden.

You only ever see "tennis" or "reading". The weather that caused it stays hidden, and the model has to infer it.

Strengths and Weaknesses

Advantages

  • Solid, well-understood probability foundation
  • Fast, efficient inference algorithms
  • Easy to interpret: every number has a meaning
  • Built for sequences, not single items
  • Works even with limited training data

Limitations

  • Strong independence assumptions
  • Weak at long-range dependencies
  • Struggles with complex language structure
  • Outperformed by Transformers on modern NLP
Where HMMs Fall Short
1

Short memory

The Markov assumption only looks one step back. Real grammar often depends on much more context.

2

Blind emissions

An emission looks at the word alone. It cannot use capital letters, prefixes, or neighboring words.

3

Features don't combine easily

Adding one new clue (like word shape) means redesigning the whole emission distribution.

These exact gaps are why Maximum Entropy Markov Models and Conditional Random Fields relax the independence rules, and why modern neural taggers replace hand-built emissions with learned representations, picking up right where this chapter ends.

Key Takeaway

An HMM guesses a hidden sequence from an observed one, using two simple ingredients: how hidden states change over time (A), and what each hidden state tends to produce (B). Ice cream reveals weather. Words reveal grammar. Sound reveals speech. Same idea, every time.

Real-World Example: Part-of-Speech Tagging
Words are the observations. Grammar tags are the hidden states.

In part-of-speech tagging, the words in a sentence are what you observe. The grammar tag behind each word (Noun, Verb, Adjective) is hidden. The HMM learns two things: how likely one tag is to follow another, and how likely a tag is to produce a certain word.

States (hidden)

Noun (N), Verb (V), Adjective (ADJ). Hidden because a plain sentence never labels its own grammar.

Observations

The actual words in the sentence. Each hidden state can produce different words.

Transitions

The chance of moving from one tag to the next. Example: a Noun is followed by a Verb 70% of the time.

Emissions

The chance a tag produces a specific word. Example: the Noun state produces "light" 40% of the time.

The three tags and how they connect

Bright arrows are the transitions and word choices the winning tagging actually uses. Dim arrows (including the small loops, a tag repeating itself) are the other routes the model also checks and rejects.

The HMM tests many possible tag sequences for this sentence. For each one, it multiplies the transition probabilities (how tags follow each other) with the emission probabilities (how tags produce words). The sequence with the highest result wins.

P(tags, words) = π(t₁) · P(t₂|t₁) · P(t₃|t₂) · P(t₄|t₃) · P(w₁|t₁) · P(w₂|t₂) · P(w₃|t₃) · P(w₄|t₄)
One factor for every tag change, one factor for every word produced.

A weak word-to-tag match, multiplied through the whole sequence, is enough to sink it to the very bottom.

Winner: ADJ → N → V → ADJ Blue/ADJ light/N shines/V bright/ADJ

Scoring all 81 sequences by hand works for a 4-word sentence, but it grows fast: a 10-word sentence would need 3¹⁰ (nearly 60,000) sequences. The Viterbi algorithm finds the same winning sequence without ever checking them one by one.
