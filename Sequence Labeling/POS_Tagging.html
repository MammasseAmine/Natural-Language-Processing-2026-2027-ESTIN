Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging

Tag Every Word, Understand Every Sentence.
What is POS Tagging?

Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging means giving every word in a sentence its grammatical label: noun, verb, adjective, adverb, and so on. A part of speech is simply the job a word does in the sentence. Humans do this without thinking while reading. A computer, however, needs linguistic knowledge and algorithms to find the role of each word, and that is what this chapter is about.

Lexical information

  • What the word usually is, on its own
  • "run" is most often a verb
  • Some words never change: "the" is always a determiner

Contextual information

  • The job the word does in this sentence
  • "a run in my stockings" → here "run" is a noun
  • A good tagger balances both kinds of information
Why is POS Tagging Important?

POS tags describe the structure of a sentence. That structural information is the foundation of many NLP applications:

Machine Translation

Knowing word roles helps translate correctly.

Speech Recognition

Grammar helps pick the right word among similar sounds.

Sentiment Analysis

Adjectives and adverbs often carry the emotion.

Named Entity Recognition

Proper nouns point to people, places, and companies.

Dependency Parsing

Tags are the first step to a full sentence structure.

Chatbots & QA Systems

Understanding questions needs word roles ("Book a flight!").

Information Retrieval

Better search by knowing what words mean grammatically.

Text Classification & Summarization

Structure helps find what matters in a text.

Tag Sets: Which Labels Do We Use?

Before tagging, we must agree on the list of possible tags. Two standards dominate:

The Penn Treebank tagset is the historical standard for English. It was used to annotate the Wall Street Journal corpus (about 1 million words). Its 45 tags split categories by inflection, a design that fits English morphology well. For example, English verbs get six different tags:

TagMeaningExample
VBVerb, base formgo
VBDVerb, past tensewent
VBGVerb, gerund / -inggoing
VBNVerb, past participlegone
VBPVerb, present (not 3rd person)go (they go)
VBZVerb, present 3rd persongoes

Other frequent tags: DT (determiner) NN / NNS (noun sing. / plur.) NNP (proper noun) JJ (adjective) RB (adverb) PRP (pronoun)

The Universal Dependencies (UD) project created a cross-linguistic tag set of only 17 UPOS tags, designed to work the same way in every language. It now covers 200+ treebanks in more than 100 languages. All six Penn verb tags merge into one VERB tag; details like tense are stored as separate morphological features instead.

Choose Penn Treebank when…

  • You work on English only
  • You use existing English tools (NLTK, spaCy English)
  • Your task needs fine verb distinctions (tense, person)
  • You need compatibility with older resources

Choose Universal Dependencies when…

  • You build multilingual systems
  • You work with non-English languages
  • Cross-language consistency is the priority
  • You use UD treebanks for parsing
How Do We Tag Automatically? Four Generations

Tagging techniques evolved from hand-written rules to deep neural networks. Click each generation to explore it:

Rule-Based Taggers

The earliest systems use rules written by hand by language experts, so they do not learn from data. A dictionary first lists all possible tags for a word; then rules (the Constraint Grammar approach) remove the wrong ones using context. Example rule: "if the previous word is an article like 'the', remove the verb tag."

Example rules

RuleAssigned tag
Word ends with "-ly"Adverb (RB)
Word ends with "-ing"Verb gerund (VBG)
Word starts with a capital letterProper noun (NNP)
Word comes after "the"Probably a noun

"The boy is running quickly" → running ends with "-ing" → VBG; quickly ends with "-ly" → RB.

Advantages

  • Easy to understand
  • No training data needed
  • Every decision is transparent

Disadvantages

  • Hard to maintain
  • Cannot cover every case of a language
  • Weak on ambiguous words

Statistical Taggers: Learning from Data

Instead of writing rules, statistical taggers learn probabilities from a tagged corpus: a collection of sentences where every word already has its correct tag, like

The/DT cat/NN sleeps/VBZ  ·  Students/NNS study/VBP NLP/NNP  ·  She/PRP reads/VBZ books/NNS

The goal: find the most probable sequence of tags for a whole sentence. Two probabilities are estimated by counting in the corpus:

Emission probability: P(word | tag)

How likely is this word, given this tag? If the tag NN appears 100 times, and "cat" is tagged NN 10 of those times: P(cat | NN) = 10 / 100 = 0.10

Transition probability: P(tag₂ | tag₁)

How likely does one tag follow another? If DT appears 600 times and is followed by NN 500 times: P(NN | DT) = 500 / 600 ≈ 0.83, so a noun very often follows a determiner.

Hidden Markov Model (HMM)

In an HMM, the words are observed but the tags are hidden, so the model must guess the hidden sequence. It combines both probabilities to pick the best tags: Best tags = argmax P(Words | Tags) × P(Tags)

The Viterbi algorithm

Trying every possible tag sequence is far too expensive. Viterbi uses dynamic programming: it moves word by word, keeps only the best path to each tag, stores the best previous state, then backtracks to recover the winning sequence. Step through it yourself:

Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt)

MaxEnt taggers go one step further: they predict the tag by combining many features at once: the current word, the previous and next words, suffixes, capitalization, and the previous predicted tag. Example: the word "Running" ends with "-ing" + starts with a capital + previous word is "is" → prediction: VBG. An HMM cannot mix independent features like this.

Discriminative Machine Learning: CRF

Conditional Random Fields (CRF) became one of the most successful sequence-labeling models. Unlike an HMM, which mainly looks at the previous tag, a CRF can use arbitrary, overlapping features and considers the whole sentence before assigning tags. It can ask questions like: Does the word end in "-ly"? Is it capitalized? What is the word two positions ahead?

Why CRFs work so well

  • Uses many features at the same time
  • Reduces the label bias problem
  • Predictions are globally consistent over the sentence
  • Excellent for any sequence-labeling task

Deep Learning: No More Handcrafted Features

All previous models needed features designed by humans. Neural networks learn useful representations automatically from data, and capture long-distance context far better than HMMs. Today's state-of-the-art taggers are all neural.

RNN / LSTM & BiLSTM

  • RNNs / LSTMs read the sentence word by word and remember earlier context
  • A BiLSTM reads it in both directions (forward + backward)
  • Each word gets a fully contextual representation before it is classified
  • The most popular neural architecture for POS tagging

Transformers (BERT)

  • Use self-attention: every word looks at every other word at the same time
  • Attention weighs how important each word is for each other word
  • Reach near-perfect, human-level tagging accuracy

Key Takeaway

POS tagging gives every word its grammatical role. It is the first structural step toward understanding a sentence. Its main enemy is ambiguity, and its history is the history of NLP itself: from handwritten rules, to counted probabilities (HMM + Viterbi), to feature-rich learners (MaxEnt, CRF), to neural networks (BiLSTM, Transformers) that learn everything from data.
