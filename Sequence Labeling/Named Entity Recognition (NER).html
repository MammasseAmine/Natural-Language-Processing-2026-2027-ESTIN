Named Entity Recognition (NER)

Finding Who, What, and Where – Hidden in Plain Text
What is Named Entity Recognition?

Named Entity Recognition (NER) is the task of finding names in a text and labeling what kind of thing each name is. A name can be a person, a company, a place, a date, or an amount of money. NER is one of the first steps in turning free text into facts a computer can search, sort, or store.

How a Computer Marks Entity Borders: BIOES Tags

Some entities are more than one word, like "Tim Cook" or "New York". A computer needs a way to mark where an entity starts and where it ends. A simple B/I/O scheme only marks the start – it cannot tell a model where a name stops. The BIOES scheme fixes that with two extra tags:

B – Beginning of a multi-word entity
I – Inside, continues the entity
O – Outside, not part of any entity
E – End, the last word of a multi-word entity
S – Single, a one-word entity on its own

Marking the exact end (E) and one-word entities (S) separately gives a model sharper boundaries – it no longer has to guess whether the next word still belongs to the same name.

"Tim" opens the name, "Cook" continues it, "Jr." closes it – three tags for one three-word entity. "Sam" stands alone, so it gets a single S tag instead of a B. This is why NER is really a word-by-word labeling task, not just a search for names.

Why NER Matters

Smarter Search

Search engines use NER to know that "Paris Hilton" is a person, not a city with a hotel.

Finance & News

Trading systems scan news for company names and money amounts in real time to react fast.

Privacy Protection

Hospitals use NER to find and hide patient names in medical records automatically.

Chatbots

A booking bot needs NER to pull the city, date, and traveler name out of one sentence.

Entities can hide inside entities

"Bank of America" is one organization, but it contains the word "America", which is also a place. A good tagger must see the whole phrase, not just the pieces.

Where does the name end?

"New York Times" is one newspaper – a single ORG entity. Split it in the wrong place and a model might see two separate entities instead: a place ("New York") and a random word ("Times"). Getting the boundary right matters as much as getting the type right.

Messy, everyday text

Capital letters are a big clue for NER – casual texting removes that clue completely.

Key Takeaway

NER turns free text into structured facts by finding names and labeling their type. The hard part is never spelling the name – it is deciding what kind of thing it is and where it starts and ends, using context, not just capital letters. That is why every generation of NLP, from simple lists to transformers, has tried to solve it in a smarter way.
