Distributed Representations

From symbols to vectors: how machines learn the meaning of words
1. The Shift: From IDs to Meanings

In early Natural Language Processing (NLP), computers treated words as simple, isolated symbols. Today, we use Distributed Representations (also called Word Embeddings) to give computers a real sense of what words mean.

The Old Way: One-Hot Encoding

Every word is just a unique ID, surrounded by zeros.

Cat = [1, 0, 0, 0]   Dog = [0, 1, 0, 0]
Huge & Empty: Wastes memory (mostly zeros).
No Meaning: The computer cannot see that "Cat" and "Dog" are related animals.

The New Way: Distributed Vectors

Words are dense lists of numbers representing features.

Cat = [0.34, -0.12, 0.89]   Dog = [0.31, -0.10, 0.85]
Compact: Much smaller, dense lists of numbers.
Smart: Similar numbers mean the words have similar meanings!
cat = [0.34, -0.12, 0.89, 0.55, ...]

No single number in that list "means" anything on its own. Meaning only shows up when all the numbers work together – that is why it's called distributed: the meaning is spread out, not stored in one place.

Dense

Almost every number in the vector is non-zero.

Low-Dimensional

Usually 50 to 1000 numbers, far fewer than the vocabulary size.

Similarity-Aware

Words with close meanings get close vectors.

Learned

The numbers are discovered automatically from data, not written by hand.

2. One-Hot Encoding vs Distributed Representation

A one-hot vector puts a single "1" in one spot and "0" everywhere else. For the vocabulary {cat, dog, car, house}:

cat = [1,0,0,0] dog = [0,1,0,0] car = [0,0,1,0] house = [0,0,0,1]

Every pair of one-hot vectors is exactly the same distance apart. The model cannot tell that "cat" is closer to "dog" than to "car".

3. The Distributional Hypothesis
"You shall know a word by the company it keeps." – J.R. Firth

The idea is simple: words that show up in similar sentences tend to mean similar things.

The cat is sleeping. The dog is sleeping.

Because "cat" and "dog" appear in the same kind of sentence, their vectors end up close together. Every method in this chapter is really just a different way of turning this one idea into numbers.

4. The Vector Space Model

Each word becomes a point in space. Words close together on the map are close in meaning:

Notice how "cat" and "dog" sit almost on top of each other, far from "car" and "truck" – which form their own neighborhood. Nearby points always mean related words.

5. Counting Context: Windows & Co-occurrence

To use the distributional hypothesis, we need to define "context" with numbers. A context window of size k takes the k words to the left and right of a target word.

Sliding this window across a whole corpus and counting how often each word pair appears together builds a co-occurrence matrix. Each row becomes a first, raw vector for a word – built only from usage, with no manual labeling.

A first sign of trouble: this matrix is still huge – |V| × |V| – and mostly zeros, just like one-hot vectors. It's a step forward, not the final destination.
6. From Counts to Dense Vectors: PMI & SVD

Raw counts are dominated by very common words like "the" or "a," which appear everywhere and drown out the meaningful pairs (like espresso ↔ machine).

Pointwise Mutual Information (PMI)

PMI re-weights each count by how much more often two words appear together than random chance would predict:

PMI(w, c) = log₂ [ P(w, c) / (P(w) · P(c)) ]

Because PMI misbehaves for pairs that never co-occur, in practice we clip negative values to zero – this is called Positive PMI (PPMI): PPMI = max(PMI, 0).

Dimensionality Reduction (SVD)

The PPMI matrix is re-weighted but still huge and sparse. Truncated Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) factorizes it into small, dense vectors. This full pipeline – count → PPMI → SVD – is known as Latent Semantic Analysis (LSA).

M ≈ U · Σ · Vᵀ  →  keep the top d values → each word gets a dense vector of size d

Before Reduction

  • Sparse, huge (|V| ≈ 10⁴–10⁶)
  • One axis per context word
  • Mostly zeros
  • Common words dominate distances

After Reduction

  • Dense, small (d ≈ 50–300)
  • Axes have no single label (latent)
  • Almost every entry non-zero
  • Captures patterns compactly
This "count, re-weight, factorize" pipeline was the first successful route to distributed word vectors – long before the newer, prediction-based neural methods.
7. Word Embeddings

A word embedding is the most popular kind of distributed representation: a learned dense vector for each word, stored as one row of an embedding matrix.

Vocabulary size

e.g. 10,000 words

Embedding dimension

e.g. 300 numbers per word

Matrix shape

10,000 × 300 – each row is one word's vector

Well-known, production-grade tools that produce this kind of vector include:

Word2Vec GloVe FastText

Their internal training recipes are covered in a dedicated course – what matters here is that all three output the same kind of object: one dense vector per word, placed so that geometric distance mirrors semantic distance.

Semantic Relationships Emerge Automatically

One striking way to test an embedding space is the analogy task: given "A is to B as C is to ___", vector arithmetic fills in the blank.

King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen
Paris − France + Germany ≈ Berlin

Nobody programs these relationships by hand – they appear naturally once the vectors are trained on enough text, and the analogy task simply reveals that they were learned.

The Ambiguity Problem

A traditional embedding gives one fixed vector per word, even when the word has several meanings – for example "bank" (financial institution) and "bank" (river side) share the same vector, blending both senses into one blurry average. This is exactly the gap that contextual embeddings are built to close: models like ELMo, BERT, RoBERTa, GPT, and LLaMA compute a fresh vector for each word based on the sentence around it, so the same word can mean different things in different places.

8. The Geometry of Meaning

Cosine Similarity

Raw distance is misleading because it is dominated by vector length, which mostly reflects word frequency. Instead, we measure the angle between two vectors:

cos(u, v) = (u · v) / (‖u‖ ‖v‖)

cos(θ) = 1.000 identical direction
  • cos = 1 → same direction, highly similar
  • cos = 0 → unrelated (orthogonal), as with one-hot vectors
  • cos = -1 → opposite meaning (rare for real word vectors)

Semantic Clustering

When projected down to 2D or 3D for visualization (with PCA or t-SNE – the real vectors actually live in 50–300 dimensions), related words form visible neighborhoods: fruits together, countries together, professions together.

Linear Analogy Structure

The most celebrated property: relationships between words become simple straight-line offsets in vector space.

These directions encode real relations – gender, verb tense, capital-of, plural-of – which downstream models can reuse and combine.

Key Takeaway: Geometry is what turns vectors into meaning. Angle (cosine similarity) tells us how alike two words are, nearby points cluster into semantic neighborhoods, and straight-line offsets between points capture real relationships like gender or capital-of. This geometric structure – not any single number – is what makes distributed representations useful.

9. Static vs Contextual Embeddings

9.1 Static Embeddings

  • One fixed vector per word, no matter the sentence
  • Simple lookup: φ(w) = row of matrix E
  • Examples: Word2Vec, GloVe, FastText
  • "bank" gets the same vector in every sentence

9.2 Contextual Embeddings

  • Vector is computed from the whole sentence
  • φ(word, context) = output of a neural network
  • Examples: ELMo (bi-LSTM), BERT, RoBERTa, GPT (Transformer)
  • Same word gets a different vector per sentence

static: one fixed vector for every sense – contextual: a fresh, separate vector for each occurrence

Limitations of Static Embeddings

Polysemy

One vector must awkwardly average all senses of a word – the senses blur together.

Out-of-vocabulary

Typos, new words, and rare technical terms get no vector at all.

Encoded bias

Vectors inherit and can amplify the biases already present in the training text.

These limits are exactly what motivate contextual representations, built by deep, sequence-aware models such as ELMo and BERT – the subject of the next course.
10. Choosing the Right Size d

The embedding dimension d is a key design choice, with a real tradeoff:

Too Low

Not enough room to capture the real complexity of relationships. Different words are squeezed into the same region – like compressing an image too much.

Just Right

Enough capacity to encode the true structure of the data, without memorizing noise. The best value depends on vocabulary size, task complexity, and how much training data is available.

Too High

The model starts memorizing instead of generalizing. Many dimensions become redundant, computation grows, and overfitting risk increases.

A simple information-theoretic lower bound: to give N words unique IDs we need at least

d ≥ log₂(N) / log₂(b)   (b = distinguishable states per dimension)

In practice d is set much higher than this bound, because we want the space to encode relationships between words, not just tell them apart.

11. How Are They Learned?

Embeddings are learned automatically from huge amounts of plain text, with no manual labeling, using a "fake" or proxy task that is later thrown away.

  1. Initialization – every word starts with a random vector.
  2. Prediction – a model slides across the text and tries to predict a word from its context (or the context from a word).
  3. Update – backpropagation nudges the vectors so predictions get slightly better each time.
  4. Result – after millions of updates, words that share contexts are pulled closer together. The prediction task is discarded; only the learned vectors are kept.

14. Bias, Fairness & Ethics

Because distributed representations learn from human-written text, they inherit – and can amplify – the biases already present in that text: gender, racial, and cultural stereotypes. For example, embedding spaces have been shown to place "doctor" closer to "he" than "she," and to link "programmer" more with male terms.

Researchers work on debiasing – for example, subtracting a learned "gender direction" from the vectors, or training the model adversarially to remove sensitive information. Complete debiasing remains an open challenge, so responsible use requires ongoing auditing, transparent reporting, and attention to the wider impact on society.

Key Takeaway

Distributed representations turned words from empty symbols into points in a rich geometric space, where distance and direction carry real meaning. That single shift – from IDs to vectors – is the foundation everything in modern NLP is built on, from search engines to today's large language models.
