FastText

Word Embeddings with Subwords
Introduction

FastText is a word representation and text classification technique developed by Facebook AI Research (FAIR) in 2016. It was designed to improve traditional word embeddings by incorporating information from subwords (character n-grams).

Unlike traditional approaches that learn one vector for each word, FastText represents a word as a collection of character sequences. This enables the model to capture morphological information and handle rare or unseen words effectively.

  Key Idea: A word is not treated as a single unit. Instead, it is represented using multiple character n-grams.

Motivation – Why Subwords?

Many languages contain rich morphology: the same word appears in many forms because of prefixes, suffixes, or inflections. For example:

play playing played player

Traditional embeddings learn a different vector for each form, so these related words may not share enough information. FastText fixes this by learning representations for subword units:

playing  =  play + ing

The model learns both the root and the suffix, so similar words automatically share knowledge.

What is a Character N-Gram? – Try It Yourself

An n-gram is a sequence of n consecutive characters. FastText first wraps the word in boundary symbols < and >, then slides a window of size n across it. Type any word and change n to see the pieces appear:

Careful detail: the piece her inside where is not the same unit as the whole word <her> , the boundary symbols keep them apart. In real FastText, all sizes from 3 to 6 are used together.

Core Concept – From Pieces to a Vector

Instead of assigning one vector to one word, FastText assigns vectors to the character n-grams. The final word vector is simply obtained by adding all subword vectors together. Let w be the word, G(w) the set of its n-grams (including the full word itself), and zg the vector of n-gram g:

v(w)  =  Σg ∈ G(w) zg
word vector = sum of the vectors of its character n-grams (in practice the implementation averages, same geometry, just rescaled)
FastText Architecture – Training Pipeline
1 Collect a large text corpus.
2 Split words into character n-grams.
3 Initialize the n-gram vectors.
4 Train with a Skip-Gram or CBOW objective.
5 Update the subword vectors through backpropagation.
6 Generate the final word embeddings.

Training follows the same idea as Skip-Gram: given a target word, predict the words around it. Example, in "The cat sits on the mat", the target cat should predict The and sits. The only difference: the score now comes from the sum of subword vectors, and backpropagation updates the pieces, not the whole word.

The memory trick (hashing): a big corpus produces tens of millions of different n-grams – too many to store one vector each. FastText hashes every n-gram into one of about 2 million buckets (FNV-1a hash). Pieces that land in the same bucket share a vector. A few collisions happen, but quality barely drops, a smart memory-for-accuracy trade.

Why 3 to 6? Pieces shorter than 3 (like re, he) appear everywhere and carry almost no meaning. Six characters is long enough for most real morphemes: -ation (6), -ment (4), pre- (3). Languages with longer morphemes can extend the range. Bonus: even before training, related words already overlap in pieces, so the model starts from a better position and converges faster.

The Superpower: Unseen Words (OOV)

Word2Vec fails on a word it never saw during training, it returns nothing. FastText simply rebuilds the word from its pieces. Watch it happen:

1. The model was trained on Wikipedia. It saw biology, informatics, information… but never bioinformatics.
2. Cut the new word into pieces: bio inf info form mati atics … – almost all of them were seen during training!
3. Add the piece vectors: u = Σ zg. A brand-new vector exists, for free.
4. Its nearest neighbours? biology and informatics, exactly what makes sense. ✅ The same trick fixes typos: gouvernment lands right next to government, which is gold for social-media text.
But Wait… What if a Subword Itself is Unseen?

Good question: we said an OOV word is rebuilt from its n-grams… but what if one of those n-grams was never seen during training either? The answer is elegant: in FastText, an n-gram can never be "out of vocabulary" , because n-gram vectors are not stored in a vocabulary at all. They are stored in a hash table.

Every possible n-gram , seen or unseen , is passed through the FNV-1a hash function, which maps it to one of the ~2 million buckets. The bucket index is the address of its vector. So looking up a never-seen n-gram never fails: it simply lands in some bucket and returns the vector stored there.

Case 1 – Seen n-gram

Its bucket vector was updated many times during training. The vector is meaningful and high-quality. This is the normal case.

Case 2 – Unseen n-gram

It hashes to a bucket that was probably trained by other colliding n-grams. The returned vector is a bit noisy – but since the word vector averages many n-grams, this noise mostly cancels out.

Case 3 – Totally foreign word

A Chinese word in an English model: all its n-grams are unseen, so all buckets return unrelated vectors. FastText still outputs a vector – but it's essentially meaningless noise. A vector always exists; a useful vector needs at least some shared n-grams.

  In short: there is no such thing as an OOV n-gram in FastText. The hashing trick guarantees every character sequence maps to some vector. The real question is not "does a vector exist?" (always yes) but "was that bucket trained on related data?" , and the more n-grams a word shares with the training corpus, the better its final vector.

Best Friend of Rich-Morphology Languages

Arabic example

كتب · كاتب · مكتبة · مكتوب

All built on the root ك-ت-ب (writing). Shared character patterns → shared meaning, learned automatically.

Where it shines

Arabic, Turkish, German, Finnish, Russian, Tamazight… any language where words carry many prefixes, suffixes and inflections. Rare forms inherit quality from frequent relatives (finissons ↔ finissaient).

No linguistics inside!

FastText uses no stemmer and no dictionary. Cutting into n-grams is purely mechanical, that's exactly why it works on any language, out of the box.

FastText as a Text Classifier

FastText is not only an embedding tool, it's also a very fast and accurate classifier (spam / not spam, fake / real news, topic labels, sentiment…). The architecture is a tiny neural network with a single hidden layer, designed for extreme speed on very large datasets. It works in 4 steps:

1

Input representation. The document becomes a bag of n-grams, both word n-grams and character n-grams.

2

Embedding lookup. Each n-gram is mapped to its dense vector.

3

Averaging. All vectors are averaged into one fixed-size document vector, this is the hidden layer.

4

Linear classification. The document vector is multiplied by a weight matrix, then softmax gives class probabilities.

Why is it so efficient?

No deep layers, no convolutions, no attention: just a lookup, an average and one matrix multiplication. That's why FastText trains and predicts in milliseconds, even on millions of documents.

Strengths & Limits – Click the Cards
Unseen words click to flip
Any word gets a vector from its pieces – even typos and brand-new words. Word2Vec would return nothing.
Speed & size click to flip
Trains and predicts in milliseconds, with moderate memory. Perfect for mobile, edge devices and huge datasets.
Low-resource languages click to flip
With little data, sharing pieces between words squeezes maximum value out of every example.
One vector per word click to flip
"bank" (money) and "bank" (river) get the same vector. Words with several meanings stay a problem.
No context click to flip
The vector never changes with the sentence. Transformers (BERT…) read the whole context and win here.
Short-sighted click to flip
Relations between far-apart words in a text (long-range dependencies) are not modeled at all.

Key Takeaway

FastText's whole magic fits in one sentence: a word = the sum of its character pieces. That one idea gives free vectors for unseen words, robustness to typos, automatic morphology sharing across languages, and blazing-fast classification – all with a simple, cheap model. Its price: one fixed vector per word, blind to context. When context matters, hand over to transformers.
