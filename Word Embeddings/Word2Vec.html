Word2Vec

How a computer learns the meaning of a word
What is Word2Vec?

Word2Vec is a method that teaches a computer to turn words into lists of numbers, called vectors. It was created by Tomas Mikolov and his team at Google in 2013.

The goal is simple: give every word a vector so that:

Similar Meaning = Similar Vector

Words like "king" and "queen" get vectors that are close together.

Same Context = Similar Vector

Words used in similar sentences end up close in vector space.

Meaning Becomes Math

Relationships between words can be added and subtracted like numbers.

The Core Idea

Word2Vec is built on one simple rule: words that appear near the same words usually have similar meanings. The words around a word are called its context.

The neural network learns vectors so that target words can predict – or be predicted by – their context words.

Two Architectures

Word2Vec can be trained in two opposite ways. Both use the same idea of context, just in reverse directions.

Switch between the two models

CBOW

Predicts the missing word from the words around it.

Example
"The ___ sits on the mat" + (The, sits, on, the) → predicts cat
  • Fast to train
  • Works well on large datasets
  • Good for frequent words
  • Weaker for rare words
  • Blends context into one average

Skip-Gram

Predicts the context words from one target word.

Example
Input: cat → predicts: The, sits, on, the
  • Better for rare words
  • High-quality embeddings
  • Captures meaning well
  • Slower to train
  • More computation needed
The Neural Network Inside

Word2Vec uses a small, shallow neural network with just three layers. It looks complicated, but it is one of the simplest networks in deep learning.

Example: with a vocabulary of 10,000 words and an embedding size of 300, the input and output layers each have 10,000 units, and the hidden layer has only 300. After training, the hidden layer weights become the word embeddings – that hidden layer is the real prize, not the output.

The Math Behind It

Word2Vec tries to make the probability of the real context words as high as possible. For Skip-Gram, the goal is:

J = (1/T) · Σ Σ log P( context word | target word )

T = total number of words in the text.

This probability is computed with the Softmax function:

P(context | target) = exp(v'·v_target) / Σ exp(v'_j · v_target)
The problem
That bottom sum (the denominator) must add up over every single word in the vocabulary – this can be hundreds of thousands of words. Doing this at every training step is far too slow. This is why Word2Vec needed a shortcut.
Speed Tricks: Making Training Fast

To avoid the slow Softmax sum, Word2Vec uses one of two clever shortcuts.

Compare the two speed tricks

Instead of updating all vocabulary words, only update:

  • 1 real (positive) context word
  • K random "wrong" (negative) words – usually 5 to 20

This turns one giant multi-class problem into a few small yes/no problems. Negative words are picked using each word's frequency raised to the power 3/4, so rare words also get a fair chance of being chosen. Cost drops from O(V) to O(K). This is the most widely used trick.

Instead of a flat list of all words, arrange the vocabulary as a binary tree (a Huffman tree).

  • Each word sits at a leaf of the tree
  • Reaching a word means making a left/right decision at each node
  • Only the path to that one word needs to be checked

Cost drops from O(V) to O(log V) – a huge saving for large vocabularies.

Subsampling of Frequent Words

Very common words like "the", "and", "of" appear everywhere but teach the model very little. Word2Vec randomly skips some of their occurrences during training.

P(discard word) = 1 − √(t / frequency(word))

t is a small threshold, usually 0.00001.

Example: if a word appears in 1% of all training samples, it has about a 68% chance of being skipped each time it shows up. Result: faster training and better vectors for rarer, more meaningful words.

What Comes Out: Word Embeddings

After training, every word becomes a dense list of numbers – a vector.

WordEmbedding (example)
King[0.72, -0.12, 0.44, …]
Queen[0.69, -0.09, 0.41, …]
Man[0.55, 0.12, 0.33, …]

Key Properties

  • Dense: usually 100–300 numbers (vs. 100,000+ for one-hot encoding)
  • Continuous: real numbers, not just 0 or 1
  • Geometric: similar meaning = close together in space

Measuring Similarity

To compare two word vectors, Word2Vec uses Cosine Similarity – it measures the angle between two vectors, not their length.

cos(A, B) = (A · B) / (‖A‖ · ‖B‖)

Where Word2Vec Is Used
Limitations
  • Gives one fixed vector per word
  • Cannot handle polysemy (a word with several meanings)
  • Embeddings do not change with context
  • Struggles with words never seen during training (out-of-vocabulary)
  • Cannot directly capture the meaning of a full sentence
Example: the word "bank"
Word2Vec gives the exact same vector for "bank" in both sentences:
River bank 🏞️ Financial bank 🏦
Modern contextual models like BERT, GPT, and RoBERTa fix this – they give a word a different vector depending on the sentence it appears in.
Key Takeaway

Word2Vec turns words into numbers so machines can work with meaning, not just spelling. By learning from context – CBOW predicting a word from its neighbors, or Skip-Gram predicting neighbors from a word – it produces vectors where distance and direction actually mean something. Its main limit is that each word gets only one fixed vector, which is exactly the problem that modern contextual models were built to solve.
