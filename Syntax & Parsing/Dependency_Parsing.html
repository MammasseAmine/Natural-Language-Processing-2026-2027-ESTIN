Dependency Parsing

One arrow per word. One label per arrow. Meaning almost for free.
The question this chapter asks

In the previous course we asked: how do words group into phrases? That gave us trees made of boxes inside boxes.

This one asks a sharper question: which word depends on which? We draw one arrow per word and put a label on every arrow. When we finish, the meaning of the sentence is almost already there.

Definition

Dependency parsing is the task of finding the grammatical structure of a sentence by linking words in pairs. Each link joins a head (the word in charge) to a dependent (the word that modifies or completes it). All the links together form one rooted tree over the words.

Two ways to look at the same sentence

5 words, 5 arrows. The verb reads is the top word. Who does what to whom is readable at a glance.

Same sentence, but the tree adds new nodes (S, NP, VP) that are not words. To find the subject you must first walk down the branches.

Constituency tree

Builds phrases. Words sit only at the bottom. Extra nodes are invented. Good for showing how a sentence is put together.

Dependency tree

Builds word-to-word links. Nothing is invented: n words give exactly n arrows. Good for showing who did what to whom.

Why an NLP pipeline cares

Most later tasks (relation extraction, sentiment targets, question answering) need predicate-argument structure (the verb and its participants), not phrase borders. A dependency tree hands you that directly: the verb is the head, and its arguments hang off it with named arrows. From a constituency tree you must first apply extra head rules to recover the same facts.

The arc: head, dependent, label

A dependency tree is made of one thing repeated: the arc. Learn the arc and you have learned the whole data structure. Formally an arc is a triple (head, dependent, label), and the arrow always points from head to dependent.

The five words you must know

Head

The governing word. It controls the pair and decides what the phrase behaves like.

Dependent

The word that modifies or completes its head.

Relation

The name of the grammatical job: subject, object, determiner…

Root

The one word with no head. Usually the main verb.

Arc

The directed edge itself, drawn head → dependent.

How do you decide which word is the head?

There is no single universal rule, but four tests settle most cases. Try them on the pair "red car".

The one honest disagreement

In "in the garden", is the head the preposition in or the noun garden?

garden is the head. UD's rule: content words are heads, function words are dependents. That single decision is what makes trees comparable across languages that have no prepositions at all.

A useful consequence

If a sentence has n words, a legal tree has exactly n arcs: one per word, counting the arc from ROOT. So a parser never has to decide how many arrows to draw, only where they go. That is why parsing can be written as: pick one head for every word.

Projective vs non-projective

Projective

Every arc can be drawn above the sentence without crossing another arc. Equivalent test: every word sits under its head with no outsiders in between.

Non-projective

At least two arcs cross. This happens when a word is pulled far from its head. It is common in Czech, Arabic, German, Latin, and in spoken language everywhere.

on the issue belongs to hearing, but it appears after scheduled. The arc hearing → issue and the arc scheduled → today must cross.

Why this word matters so much

The fast parsing algorithms you will meet in a moment can only produce projective trees. If your language needs crossings, you must either repair the tree afterwards (pseudo-projective parsing), add special transitions (swap), or switch to a graph-based parser, which has no such limit. English is about 99% projective; Czech is closer to 77%. So the choice of algorithm really is a choice about which languages you can serve.

The distinction that trips students up: obj vs obl

An object is a bare noun phrase the verb needs. An oblique is a noun phrase attached through a preposition, and it is usually optional.

Removing an object usually breaks the sentence. Removing an oblique rarely does.

How parsers actually build the tree

Transition-based parsing, step by step

The parser holds three things: a stack of words it is still working on, a buffer of words it has not read yet, and the arcs it has already committed to. It repeats one of three moves (this version is called arc-standard):

SHIFT

Move the next buffer word onto the stack. "I am not ready to decide yet."

LEFT-ARC(l)

The top of the stack is head of the word below it. Add the arc, delete the lower word.

RIGHT-ARC(l)

The word below is head of the top word. Add the arc, delete the top word.

Where the learning happens

Notice that nothing above says which move to take. That is the machine-learning part: a classifier looks at the current stack and buffer and predicts the next move. Old parsers used a linear model over hand-written features (top-of-stack word, its part of speech, the next buffer word…). Modern ones feed the same configuration to a neural network. Training data is made by replaying gold trees to recover the correct move sequence. This is called an oracle.

Why dependencies won

Constituency parsing dominated the 1990s. Dependency parsing dominates today. Four reasons.

Free word order stops being a problem

In Arabic, Russian, Latin or Turkish the subject can appear almost anywhere. A phrase-structure grammar needs a new rule for every order. An arc does not care about position: nsubj is nsubj whether the subject comes before or after the verb.

Meaning is one step away

"Who did what to whom" is read straight off the arcs around the verb. From a constituency tree you must first apply head-percolation rules to recover the same information.

Speed

A transition-based parser runs in linear time, O(n). CKY constituency parsing is O(n³·|G|). On web-scale corpora that gap decides what is possible at all.

One scheme for all languages

Universal Dependencies gives the same label set to 100+ languages. Train on one, transfer to another, and compare every result on one scale.

Key takeaway

A dependency tree says one thing about every word: who is my head, and what job do I do for it? Answer that n times and you have the syntax of the sentence. No invented nodes, no phrase boxes, and the meaning sitting one step away.
