Constituency Parsing

How a machine turns a flat line of words into a tree of nested phrases, and why translation, question answering and LLMs need that tree

The lesson
Meaning depends on structure. A machine that only sees a flat list of words cannot tell these two readings apart. Constituency parsing recovers the hidden tree, so later steps (machine translation, question answering, information extraction, summarization, dialogue systems, even large language models) can use it.

What Is a Constituent?

A constituent is a word or group of words that acts as one single unit inside a bigger structure. It can be a word, a phrase, a clause, or the whole sentence. In “The old man fed the pigeons”, the words “the old man” form one unit: a noun phrase. But “man fed the” is not a unit at all. How do linguists check? With four classic tests. Click each card.

The Five Common Phrase Types

Every phrase is named after its most important word: its head. A phrase built around a noun is an NP; around a verb, a VP; and so on.

Where Trees Come From: Context-Free Grammar

Constituency parsing is built on Phrase Structure Grammar, better known as a Context-Free Grammar (CFG): a set of rewrite rules that says which structures a language allows. A CFG has exactly four ingredients:

IngredientSymbolMeaningExample
Non-terminalsNphrase & POS labelsS, NP, VP, Det, N, V
TerminalsΣthe actual wordsthe, dog, cat, chased
RulesRA → Î²  (rewrite A as Î²)VP → V NP
Start symbolSwhere every derivation beginsS
Why “context-free”?
A rule like NP → Det N can be applied wherever an NP appears; its neighbors (the context) never matter. This is what keeps CFGs simple and fast, and it is exactly the property that chart algorithms exploit.

A tiny toy grammar: six rules are enough to generate real sentences:

S → NP VP NP → Det N VP → V NP Det → the | a N → dog | cat V → chased | saw

Treebanks & Penn Notation

Where do parsers learn from? From a Treebank: a corpus of sentences hand-annotated with parse trees. Treebanks are essential both for training parsers and for evaluating them. Famous examples: the Penn Treebank (millions of words with POS tags and constituency trees, built at the University of Pennsylvania, the most influential resource in the field), the Chinese Treebank, the Arabic Treebank, and the Universal Dependencies treebanks.

On disk, trees are stored as bracket notation: the same structure written with parentheses. Toggle between the two views of “The dog chased the cat”:

Penn POS tags: DT = determiner, NN = noun, VBD = past-tense verb.
(S (NP (DT The) (NN dog)) (VP (VBD chased) (NP (DT the) (NN cat))))
Same tree, two costumes
The bracket string and the drawing carry identical information: every pair of parentheses = one node of the tree. This compact format is what parsers are actually trained on.
Parsing Algorithms: Two Directions

A grammar tells us which structures are possible. A parsing algorithm does the opposite job: given a sentence and a grammar, it must decide whether the grammar can generate the sentence, and build the tree if yes. There are two natural directions. The Fix: Dynamic Programming & Chart Parsing

In an ambiguous sentence, many candidate trees share the same pieces: the NP “the dog” is the same NP in every tree that contains it. So why compute it twice? Dynamic programming computes each constituent once, stores it, and reuses it. This one idea turns exponential search into polynomial work.

Chart parsing applies this idea with a table called a chart. Positions are placed between words, and every discovered constituent is stored as a span Label(start, end), computed once and never rebuilt.

How the chart fills, step by step
The algorithm works in two passes. Pass 1: look up each single word's category (a lexical span of length 1). Pass 2: repeatedly combine two adjacent spans already in the chart into a bigger one, exactly as the grammar's rules allow, until the whole sentence is covered by S(0,5). Key idea in one line
Chart = memory. Steps 8 and 9 above never recompute NP(3,5) or NP(0,2): they just look them up. Every partial result Label(start, end) is saved once and shared by all trees, no constituent is ever rebuilt. This is the foundation of the CKY and Earley algorithms used in practice.

Takeaways

  • Constituent: a group of words acting as one unit; proven by substitution, movement, coordination, and stand-alone-answer tests.
  • Phrase types: NP, VP, PP, AdjP, AdvP, each named after its head word.
  • CFG: four ingredients (non-terminals, terminals, rules, start symbol); rules apply anywhere, regardless of context.
  • Parse tree: root S, internal category nodes, word leaves; stored as Penn-style bracket notation in treebanks.
  • Top-down vs bottom-up: guess-then-check vs build-from-words; both waste work on repeated constituents.
  • Chart parsing: dynamic programming saves every span once, making efficient algorithms like CKY possible.
